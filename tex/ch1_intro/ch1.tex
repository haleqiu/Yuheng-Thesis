\chapter{Introduction} \label{chapter:introduction}
\fancyhead[LO]{\makebox[\textwidth][r]{Chapter 1. Introduction}}

Modern robots must operate in places that no calibration lab can anticipate.
A quadrotor chasing a racing line climbs through motion blur, aggressive rotation, and rapid exposure changes within a single turn.
An autonomous delivery vehicle enters a low-light tunnel in one minute and exits into direct sunlight in the next.
A subterranean search-and-rescue robot crosses from reflective shotcrete to dust-filled, textureless drifts within a single traverse~\cite{zhao2023subt}.
A lunar rover must localize over regolith that looks nearly identical across kilometers, using a rigidly mounted stereo camera and an IMU that drifts through thermal cycles of hundreds of degrees.
None of these platforms tolerates odometry that fails silently, and none of them can afford a recalibration procedure between every mission.
Between the raw sensor stream and every downstream decision, these robots rely on \textit{spatial perception}: the continuous estimation of where the platform is, how it is moving, and what surrounds it from visual, inertial, and other onboard signals.
Together, these scenarios point to a single goal for spatial perception: state estimation that works on \textit{any platform, anywhere, anytime}, generalizing across bodies, environments, and operating conditions without per-deployment retuning.

Classical spatial perception systems approach this task as posterior inference in a factor graph or filter~\cite{barfoot2017state, cadena2016past}.
Visual correspondences, inertial preintegrations, and cross-sensor constraints each contribute a residual, and the estimator weights those residuals by the inverse covariance of their noise.
In principle, the same geometric solver can absorb any new sensor, platform, or environment simply by supplying its measurement function and its covariance.
In practice, only half of this promise holds: measurement functions transfer readily because they encode physics, but covariance does not transfer, because real-world measurement noise depends on scene content (feature density, illumination, texture), on device and mounting (IMU grade, stereo baseline, thermal profile), and on motion (vibration spectrum, rotation rate, thrust profile) in ways that offline calibration cannot predict ahead of deployment~\cite{rehder2016extending, qin2018vins}.
As a consequence, each new deployment accumulates another round of threshold tuning, covariance calibration, and hand-added heuristics, and each such iteration makes the resulting system harder to debug while leaving its generalization to unseen sensors, platforms, and environments fragile.

Deep learning offers a natural response to this covariance bottleneck.
Measurement reliability depends jointly on scene content, sensor characteristics, mounting, and motion regime in ways that are too high-dimensional and too continuous for hand-engineered noise tables to enumerate, but neural networks can learn this mapping directly from the same sensor streams the estimator already consumes and amortize the calibration effort across deployments rather than repeat it for every new platform.
Yet the way deep learning has entered spatial perception has diverged into two extremes that do not address the bottleneck.
One extreme replaces the geometric solver with end-to-end pose regression, which sacrifices the information-weighted inference that makes classical estimators data-efficient and physically interpretable.
The other extreme keeps the classical solver intact but bolts on learned features whose confidence scores have no physical scale~\cite{teed2021droid, teed2024deep}, which improves front-end robustness but leaves the covariance values hand-tuned and the factor weights manually balanced.
This dissertation pursues a narrower intervention that centers on data-driven uncertainty modeling.
Rather than redesign the geometric solver, it learns the covariance the solver consumes, so that measurement reliability is modeled explicitly from data and adapts as sensors, environments, and platforms change.

\section{Bitter Lessons from SLAM: My First Journey with AirDOS}
\label{sec:bitter_lessons}

The thesis statement in the next section did not arise in a vacuum.
It is grounded in deployment experience with classical SLAM, and in particular with AirDOS~\cite{qiu2022airdos}.
AirDOS was one of the first dynamic-object SLAM systems to model moving humans as articulated rigid bodies, using their joint kinematics as additional geometric constraints in the back-end factor-graph optimization rather than discarding human points as outliers.
AirDOS reached state-of-the-art accuracy when scene dynamics matched the conditions it was tuned on, but every shift in those dynamics exposed a new failure mode, and each failure mode was patched with another hand-added constraint, outlier rule, or covariance value.
As the number of hand-tuned factors grew, the system accumulated engineering complexity without a commensurate gain in out-of-distribution performance.
The factor graph became difficult to analyze, and generalization to unseen dynamic environments remained unpredictable.
This trajectory is consistent with a broader pattern in visual-inertial state estimation~\cite{cadena2016past}: hand-crafted noise and residual models eventually stop scaling, while methods that learn error models from data continue to improve~\cite{sutton2019bitterlesson}.
Three lessons distill this experience and motivate the thesis statement that follows.

\paragraph{Lesson 1. Hand-tuned factor uncertainties do not generalize across scenes.}
Factor-graph and filter-based state estimators are information-weighted estimators whose posterior depends on a covariance model for every sensor and residual term~\cite{barfoot2017state}.
In classical pipelines, these covariances are chosen from offline calibration~\cite{rehder2016extending}, nominal noise parameters for a specific IMU grade, or empirical sweeps on a validation set~\cite{qin2018vins}.
The AirDOS experience indicates, and related work corroborates~\cite{yang2020d3vo, muhle2023learning, solodar2023vio}, that covariance values tuned on one distribution of scenes often cause divergence on another, because hand-chosen values encode scene-specific assumptions rather than a calibrated estimate of measurement reliability.
Adding more heuristics in response to new failure modes compounds this problem, because each heuristic interacts with every other factor through the joint optimization and cannot be validated in isolation.
This outcome is the state-estimation instance of Sutton's bitter lesson~\cite{sutton2019bitterlesson}: general methods that leverage data outperform methods that encode structured human knowledge as data and computation become sufficient.

\paragraph{Lesson 2. Per-deployment retuning is not a scalable engineering model.}
The dominant cost of deploying spatial perception in the field is often not algorithm design but repeated calibration and parameter tuning when the sensor, payload, platform, or environment changes.
A typical deployment cycle exchanges days of algorithm development for weeks of data collection, threshold sweeps, covariance calibration, and factor-weight adjustment.
Empirically, this cost scales with the number of deployments rather than with the amount of data that becomes available over time.
Concretely, the bottleneck shifts from algorithmic capability to engineering throughput: each additional platform requires another iteration of data collection, threshold sweeps, and covariance calibration before the same algorithm becomes deployable, so total deployment effort grows roughly linearly with the number of platforms rather than amortizing across them.
From a systems perspective, this cycle makes human effort, rather than data, the limiting resource of spatial perception, which is precisely the scaling regime that Sutton's bitter lesson predicts will plateau~\cite{sutton2019bitterlesson}.

\paragraph{Lesson 3. Uncertainty, not accuracy, is the right quantity to learn.}
A natural response to the previous two lessons is to replace the geometric estimator with end-to-end pose regression, so that learning absorbs both the motion model and the noise model.
This approach rests on the assumption that, given enough data and computation, a sufficiently large network can subsume the geometric solver entirely and recover accurate state estimation directly from raw sensor input.
This dissertation takes a different position.
Factor graphs and filters remain the most efficient posterior estimators in spatial perception, both in computation and in the amount of data they require.
As long as the underlying physical rules, such as IMU integration, camera reprojection, and sensor extrinsics, are correctly specified, state estimation reduces to a sparse, closed-form maximum-a-posteriori problem that scales linearly with the number of variables and runs in real time on embedded hardware~\cite{barfoot2017state, forster2015imu}.
The bottleneck is therefore not the geometric solver but its covariance input: hand-tuned covariance violates the estimator's assumptions, and learned confidence values with arbitrary scale are not admissible as factor-graph covariance either.
The correct locus for learning is uncertainty itself, and the uncertainty must be metrics-aware, that is, reported in the physical units of the quantity it describes.
Under this constraint, downstream visual, inertial, and cross-sensor factors can be weighted on a common scale without manual balancing, and each learned module becomes a drop-in covariance source for the shared geometric back-end.

These three lessons motivate the thesis statement in \cref{sec:thesis_statement} and the research questions organized in \cref{sec:thesis_organization}, which operationalize ``learn the covariance, keep the geometry'' as a concrete technical program.

\section{Thesis Statement}
\label{sec:thesis_statement}

\begin{framed}
    \centering
    \textbf{Thesis Statement} \\
    \textit{Uncertainty-aware spatial perception} generalizes across unseen platforms, environments, and operating conditions without per-deployment retuning, by learning data-driven sensor models with \textit{metrics-aware covariance} and using them throughout visual, inertial, and sensor-fusion estimation.
\end{framed}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Uncertainty-aware spatial perception} means a state estimator that propagates a calibrated probabilistic belief over its pose, motion, and surroundings, and uses that belief to weight every correspondence, residual, and cross-sensor factor, so the same estimator generalizes to unseen platforms, environments, and operating conditions without re-tuning noise parameters, covariance values, fusion weights, feature thresholds, or extrinsic calibration per deployment.
    \item \textit{Metrics-aware covariance} means uncertainty with physically meaningful scale, so the estimator can use it directly to weight correspondences, residuals, and sensor factors across modalities without manual rebalancing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:thesis_organization}

The thesis statement commits to a single mechanism, learned metrics-aware uncertainty, and a single outcome, generalizable state estimation.
Turning this commitment into a research program requires identifying \emph{which} state estimation capabilities are bottlenecked by hand-tuned or scale-agnostic uncertainty, and \emph{how} each bottleneck can be relieved by a learned covariance model that remains compatible with geometric estimation.
The dissertation is organized into three parts that follow the structure in \cref{fig:ch1:thesis_overview}, each containing one or more research questions answered by the corresponding chapter.
The technical answer and headline evidence for each question are stated in \cref{sec:contributions}, and each research question is restated at the opening of the chapter that addresses it.

\subsection{\partITitle}
\label{subsec:org_part1}

The thesis first addresses vision because visual correspondences provide rich geometric constraints but fail unpredictably under real deployment conditions.
Current visual odometry systems either rely on fixed geometric uncertainty models or produce scale-agnostic confidence estimates that do not transfer across illumination shifts, texture variation, and camera domains.
This part establishes the visual uncertainty model that later parts use as one source of metric sensor reliability.

\paragraph{Research Question 1 (Metrics-Aware Visual Perception).}
\textit{How can learning-based visual perception produce covariance with physically meaningful scale that transfers across cameras, illumination, and sensor modalities without per-deployment retuning?}
Learned visual odometry systems either rely on fixed geometric noise models or emit scale-agnostic confidence scores~\cite{teed2021droid, teed2024deep}, so their uncertainty estimates cannot be used directly as factor-graph covariance.
This question is addressed in \cref{chapter:macvo} through \textbf{MAC-VO}, a stereo visual odometry system that learns metrics-aware 2D matching covariance and propagates it into 3D point covariance for keypoint selection and residual weighting.

\subsection{\partIITitle}
\label{subsec:org_part2}

The thesis next turns to inertial sensing because IMUs provide continuous motion information when visual evidence becomes unreliable.
Classical IMU preintegration uses static noise parameters, and many learning-based methods use representations that lose observability under aggressive dynamics.
This part provides the inertial counterpart to MAC-VO, a learned uncertainty model that supports transferable motion estimation when external sensing is degraded.

\paragraph{Research Question 2 (Cross-Grade Inertial Sensor Modeling).}
\textit{How can a learned inertial sensor model capture the residual noise and bias processes of a physical IMU in a way that generalizes across IMU grades, mountings, and motion regimes, and produce calibrated preintegration covariance usable by a factor graph?}
Classical preintegration uses static noise parameters and per-IMU calibration sheets that must be retuned for every new device, and existing learned IMU models either predict displacements directly without reporting uncertainty or predict uncertainty without physical scale~\cite{liu2020tlio, sun2021idol}.
This question is addressed in \cref{chapter:airimu} through \textbf{AirIMU}, a cross-device learned IMU sensor model evaluated on datasets spanning automotive, handheld, legged, and aerial platforms over more than $262\,\mathrm{km}$ of flight-like trajectories.

\paragraph{Research Question 3 (Centimeter-Level Inertial Odometry on Agile Platforms).}
\textit{How can a learning-based inertial odometry system achieve centimeter-level accuracy on agile aerial platforms using IMU measurements alone, without external sensors, control inputs, or per-platform retuning?}
Existing learning-based inertial odometry systems are predominantly developed for pedestrians or legged robots and rely on gait priors or near-constant velocity assumptions~\cite{liu2020tlio, herath2020ronin, buchanan2022deep}; when deployed on agile UAVs they lose observability of the rotation-coupled motion signals that determine thrust and attitude~\cite{CioffiRal2023}.
This question is addressed in \cref{chapter:airio} through \textbf{AirIO}, which is evaluated on UAV datasets including Blackbird and Pegasus and runs in real time on embedded platforms such as NVIDIA Jetson Orin.

\subsection{\partIIITitle}
\label{subsec:org_part3}

With visual and inertial covariance available in metric units, the thesis uses uncertainty as a common currency for fusion, adaptation, and global consistency.
Existing visual-inertial systems require manual noise and calibration choices, and they often degrade when moved to new platforms without supervised labels.

\paragraph{Research Question 4 (Self-Supervised Inertial Odometry for Arbitrary Embodiments).}
\textit{How can a learning-based inertial odometry network adapt to a new robot embodiment using only unlabeled IMU data, without motion capture, GNSS, or other externally supplied ground-truth trajectories?}
Every existing learned inertial odometry network is trained with supervised ground truth obtained from an external positioning system, so extending to a new embodiment requires collecting ground-truth trajectories that are expensive, environment-limited, or entirely unavailable on deployed robots~\cite{liu2020tlio, CioffiRal2023}.
This question is addressed in \cref{chapter:macio} through \textbf{MAC-IO}, a pretrain-then-adapt pipeline that generalizes a single learned inertial odometry network across pedestrian, legged, and aerial embodiments from unlabeled IMU alone.

\paragraph{Research Question 5 (Tuning-Free Visual-Inertial State Estimation).}
\textit{How can learned visual and inertial covariance serve as a common currency for visual-inertial initialization and online calibration without manual noise parameter retuning across environments and platforms?}
Classical visual-inertial systems require hand-tuned covariance for gravity, velocity, bias, and extrinsic parameters at initialization, and they degrade when moved to new cameras, IMUs, or robots because they lack a principled way to re-weight their factors under distribution shift~\cite{qin2018vins, geneva2020openvins, leutenegger2015keyframe}.
This question is addressed in \cref{chapter:macinit} through \textbf{MAC-I$^2$} for visual-inertial initialization and online calibration.

\subsection{Notation and Background}
\label{subsec:org_background}

\cref{chapter:background} collects the notation used throughout this dissertation for rigid-body kinematics, Lie-group perturbations, IMU preintegration, factor graphs, and covariance propagation, and reviews the classical results on which the thesis contributions build.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This dissertation makes the following contributions, organized by the three parts above.
Each contribution corresponds to one thesis chapter and one or more publications.

\paragraph{\partITitle.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1.~Chapter~\ref{chapter:macvo}~--~MAC-VO: Metrics-Aware Visual Covariance for Stereo Visual Odometry.}
    MAC-VO learns metrics-aware 2D matching covariance from visual features and propagates it into 3D point covariance with inter-axis correlations, then uses this covariance for keypoint selection and residual weighting in a two-frame stereo pose graph.
    On the TartanAir benchmark, MAC-VO reduces relative translation error by $61.9\%$ compared with DROID-SLAM and by $82.4\%$ on the lunar-surface sequence H00; on EuRoC, it outperforms all visual odometry and SLAM baselines in relative rotation error~\cite{qiu2025mac}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\partIITitle.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C2.~Chapter~\ref{chapter:airimu}~--~AirIMU: Learned IMU Sensor Model and Uncertainty Propagation.}
    AirIMU jointly learns IMU noise correction and preintegration covariance through a differentiable integrator and an on-manifold covariance propagator, producing a single learned IMU model that transfers from automotive-grade to navigation-grade devices.
    AirIMU improves IMU preintegration by $17.8\%$ on SubT-MRS~\cite{zhao2023subt}, enables a $31.6\%$ improvement in GPS-IMU pose graph optimization, and is validated on the large-scale ALTO dataset with trajectories spanning more than $262\,\mathrm{km}$~\cite{qiu2023airimu}.

    \item \textbf{C3.~Chapter~\ref{chapter:airio}~--~AirIO: Centimeter-Level Inertial Odometry on Agile UAVs.}
    AirIO shows that body-frame IMU representation and explicit attitude encoding preserve the rotation-coupled motion signals required by agile UAV dynamics, and fuses the resulting network with AirIMU preintegration through an uncertainty-aware EKF without any external sensor or control input.
    On the Blackbird drone dataset, AirIO outperforms TLIO by $66.1\%$ in absolute translation error and $71.3\%$ in relative translation error, and outperforms the thrust-augmented IMO by $56.6\%/70.2\%$; on EuRoC it improves over RoNIN and TLIO by $52.9\%$ and $54.4\%$ in absolute translation error; the system runs at $28.92\,\mathrm{ms}$ on a desktop and $74.23\,\mathrm{ms}$ on NVIDIA Jetson Orin at $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ IMU input, enabling real-time deployment on drones.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\partIIITitle.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C4.~Chapter~\ref{chapter:macio}~--~MAC-IO: Self-Supervised Inertial Odometry for New Embodiments.}
    MAC-IO learns inertial odometry for a new embodiment from unlabeled IMU through a pretrain-then-adapt pipeline: embodiment pretraining matches network velocity increments to AirIMU-corrected preintegration with a Mahalanobis loss, and PGO posterior adaptation distills sliding-window posterior velocity mean and covariance back into the network through teacher-to-student KL.
    Without any ground-truth trajectory, MAC-IO reduces average velocity error on the TLIO dataset from $0.1025\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to $0.0398\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (a $61.2\%$ reduction), and PGO adaptation at inference further reduces it to $0.0334\,\mathrm{m/s}$, becoming competitive with supervised pretrained references.

    \item \textbf{C5.~Chapter~\ref{chapter:macinit}~--~MAC-I$^2$: Metrics-Aware Visual-Inertial Initialization and Calibration.}
    MAC-I$^2$ uses metrics-aware covariance from MAC-VO and AirIMU to perform visual-inertial initialization and online camera-IMU extrinsic calibration without manual noise retuning, including scenes with extreme exposure, occlusion, and dynamic objects.
    On EuRoC, MAC-I$^2$ attains $0.418^{\circ}$ gravity direction error and $0.018\,\mathrm{m/s}$ velocity RMSE, improving over the best baseline DRT-l by $59\%$ and $74\%$ respectively, and achieves a $1.00$ initialization success rate against $0.74$, $0.48$, and $0.61$ for DRT-l, VINS-Fusion, and ORB-SLAM3; the learned visual covariance further reduces gyroscope bias error by $46.4\%$.

\end{itemize}

Publications associated with each contribution, together with the detailed role of the author and co-authors, are listed in \cref{sec:publications}.
